ElevateX Drive Performance Index

One score that shows how well a drive turned money, people and time into real impact. We call it EDPI, and every ElevateX drive gets one.

Why we built EDPI

Every ElevateX drive takes money, time and a lot of effort from our members. Until EDPI, there was no clear way to say whether one drive went better than another. A big drive is not always a better drive, and a cheap drive is not always a good one.

EDPI gives every drive one score. A drive that does exactly what it planned scores 100. It can go above 100 by getting more volunteers than planned and by working with an outside partner. The highest possible score is 115.

Did we actually achieve what we set out to do?

Did we spend wisely, and did we stay inside the fund?

How a drive is scored

Every drive follows the same five steps.

Make the plan

Before the drive, the organiser fills in six planning values. These are the targets the drive will be judged against.

Lock the plan

Once the drive starts, nobody changes these values. If targets could be changed afterwards, the score would mean nothing.

Run the drive and keep records

Receipts for the money spent, a count of the people helped, a sign-in sheet with each volunteer's hours, and a record of the impact achieved.

Enter the results

After the drive, five values are recorded.

Calculate and read the score

The result comes from the formulas below and is read against the drive's own plan.

The calculation

Four steps. After each formula there's a short note on what it does, why we need it and the thinking behind it.

Step 1: Basic figures

TotalHours = the sum of HoursEach for every volunteer

AvgHours = TotalHours ÷ Volunteers

CostPerPerson = Spent ÷ PeopleHelped

BudgetUsed = Spent ÷ Budget

What it does. Turns the raw entries into four simple figures: total volunteer hours, average hours per volunteer, money spent per beneficiary, and the share of the fund we used (1.00 means we used all of it).

Why we need it. We can't fairly compare a small drive with a big one using raw totals. Averages and ratios take the size out of the picture.

The thinking behind it. Each figure is just an addition or a division. There is no judgement in this step.

Step 2: Four scores

Each score is out of 100, except turnout, which can go higher. A higher score is always better.

Cost score

If CostPerPerson ≤ NormalCost: CostScore = 100

Otherwise: CostScore = 100 × NormalCost ÷ CostPerPerson (with the fixed settings, 5342 ÷ CostPerPerson)

What it does. Compares what the drive spent on each beneficiary with what ElevateX usually spends.

Why we need it. Money spent only means something when we know how many people it helped. Looking at total spending alone would make small drives look better than they are.

The thinking behind it. The score is the usual cost divided by the actual cost. If a drive spends twice the usual amount per person, it gets 50. If it spends less than usual, it gets full marks but nothing extra, so one very cheap drive can't hide problems somewhere else.

Budget score

If BudgetUsed ≤ OverspendStart: BudgetScore = 100

If BudgetUsed is between the two limits: BudgetScore = 100 − 100 × (BudgetUsed − OverspendStart) ÷ (OverspendZero − OverspendStart)

If BudgetUsed ≥ OverspendZero: BudgetScore = 0

What it does. Checks that the drive stayed inside its fund. With the fixed settings, the middle case loses 2 points for every extra 1% of the fund spent.

Why we need it. Going over budget takes money away from other drives, so it needs its own penalty.

The thinking behind it. There is no penalty up to 100% of the fund. After that, the score drops in a straight line and reaches 0 at 150%. We deliberately do not score underspending here. A drive that does less already loses points through cost per person and through impact. If we rewarded spending more, it would cancel out the cost score, because the extra spending and the higher cost per person would balance each other exactly.

Turnout score

TurnoutScore = 100 × Volunteers ÷ PlannedVolunteers, up to a limit of TurnoutMax (150)

What it does. Compares the number of volunteers who came with the number we expected.

Why we need it. A big turnout shows that the drive reached people and got them involved, so it should be rewarded, even when it goes beyond the plan.

The thinking behind it. Half the planned turnout gives 50, the planned turnout gives 100, and anything above that earns extra credit up to 150. The limit stops one huge crowd from taking over the whole index.

Time score

If AvgHours ≤ PlannedHours: TimeScore = 100

Otherwise: TimeScore = 100 × PlannedHours ÷ AvgHours

What it does. Compares the average hours each volunteer gave with the hours we planned.

Why we need it. If each volunteer has to give a lot of time, the drive is asking too much of its people. Fewer hours per volunteer means the work was shared well.

The thinking behind it. The score is the planned hours divided by the actual hours, so doubling the planned hours gives 50. Hours at or below the plan score 100.

Step 3: Three pillars and a bonus

Money score

MoneyScore = √(CostScore × BudgetScore)

What it does. Combines the cost score and the budget score into one score for money.

Why we need it. A drive can be cheap per person and still overspend, or stay on budget and still waste money. Doing well financially means getting both right.

The thinking behind it. The square root of the two scores multiplied together is called the geometric mean. It equals the normal average when the two scores are the same, and it falls below the normal average when they are far apart. If either score is 0, the money score is 0.

Volunteer score

VolunteerScore = 2 × TurnoutScore × TimeScore ÷ (TurnoutScore + TimeScore)

If TurnoutScore + TimeScore is 0, the volunteer score is 0.

What it does. Combines turnout and time per volunteer into one score for volunteers.

Why we need it. A big crowd is only good news if the work was shared fairly, and light hours mean little if hardly anyone came.

The thinking behind it. This is called the harmonic mean. It stays closer to the weaker of the two scores, so a strong result on one can't cover up a weak result on the other. For example, a turnout score of 150 with a time score of 50 gives 75, not the normal average of 100.

Impact score

If ImpactDone < ImpactTarget: ImpactScore = 100 × ImpactDone ÷ ImpactTarget

Otherwise: ImpactScore = 100

What it does. Measures what share of the impact target we actually reached.

Why we need it. Money and volunteers are what we put in. Impact is the reason the drive exists.

The thinking behind it. Hitting the target gives 100. The target has to be set before the drive, in the unit the drive really produces, like meals served or kilograms collected.

Partnership bonus

PartnerBonus = 10 if HasPartner = 1, otherwise 0

What it does. Adds 10 points when the drive worked with at least one outside partner.

Why we need it. Partnerships help ElevateX reach more people, but not every drive needs one.

The thinking behind it. It is a bonus on top of the main score, not one of the three pillars, so a drive without a partner doesn't lose anything.

Step 4: The final index

EDPI = 0.25 × MoneyScore + 0.25 × VolunteerScore + 0.50 × ImpactScore + PartnerBonus

What it does. Adds the three weighted pillars and the bonus to get one final number.

Why we need it. Members and organisers need a single score so they can compare drives and see whether we are improving.

The thinking behind it. A weighted sum is easy to check by hand. Impact gets half of the weight because it is the whole point of a drive. Money and volunteers share the other half equally.

Worked example

One drive, scored from start to finish. We planned for 40 volunteers working 4 hours each, with a fund of Rs. 60,000 and a target of 1,000 meals. In the end we spent Rs. 50,000, reached 800 people, had 50 volunteers who gave 150 hours in total, served 900 meals and worked with one outside partner.

Reading the score

EDPI does not come with a fixed pass mark, and that is on purpose.

  • 100 is the reference point. Every score compares the result with the plan, so 100 means the drive did what it said it would do. A score is easier to understand when it is measured against the drive's own goals and not against a number picked at random.
  • Above 100 means the drive beat its plan. This can happen through extra turnout or a partnership. Because turnout is limited and impact can't go past 100, one lucky area can't push a drive far above the rest.
  • Below 100 means something fell short, and the pillar scores say what. Look at the money, volunteer and impact scores one by one. The one furthest below 100 is the area to talk about in the debrief.
  • Compare a drive with our past drives of the same type. Because every score is measured against that drive's own plan, a rising score over time shows that we are planning better and delivering better.

We chose not to set labels like "good" or "poor" for ranges of scores. Any cut-off would be a guess until ElevateX has scored enough drives to know what a typical result looks like.

The minimum-hours check matters. A low average raises the time score, so without it a drive could add volunteers who log almost no time and look good on both turnout and time.

Why EDPI looks the way it does

  • Everything is compared with a plan. This makes drives of different sizes comparable.
  • Cost is measured per beneficiary. It shows how well money turns into help.
  • Geometric mean for money, harmonic mean for volunteers. Both stop a strong result in one area from hiding a weak result in another.
  • Impact gets half the weight. It is the purpose of the drive.
  • Scores have limits. Without limits, one extreme number could decide the whole index.
  • Partnerships are a bonus. They help, but a drive doesn't need one.

Limitations

  • The weights and limits are choices made by ElevateX. They are not the result of a statistical study, and another organisation might choose different ones.
  • The impact score uses one impact measure per drive. It can't capture how good the help was or how long it lasts.
  • Planned values are set by the organiser, so the index is only fair if targets are realistic and locked before the drive.
  • Volunteer hours are written down by people, so they should be checked against the sign-in sheet.
  • EDPI is new. We will review the settings as more drives are scored.
